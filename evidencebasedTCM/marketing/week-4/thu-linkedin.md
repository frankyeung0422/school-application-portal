## Post body (copy-paste ready)

I read 707 PubMed abstracts to build the EBTCM evidence database. Here's what surprised me.

(Not a list of "TCM works for X". A list of things that **changed my prior** after reading the literature.)

**1. The Cochrane Library has more Grade A acupuncture systematic reviews than I expected.**

I went in expecting a thin set. Found a robust body of work — migraine prophylaxis (PMID 35502924), chronic low-back pain (PMID 35725480), tension headache, post-stroke recovery. The methodology debate isn't whether acupuncture has evidence. It's whether sham-controlled designs are the right comparator. That's a more interesting argument than "does it work."

**2. The most-studied formula in the last 5 years isn't any of the classical ones. It's 連花清瘟.**

COVID-19 pulled it into ~50 SRs/meta-analyses in 3 years. The body of evidence is now larger than for any other patent formula. The clinical effect is modest (1.5–2 days symptom reduction vs. placebo) but it's the highest-evidence-density formula on the platform, including older classics like 桂枝湯 with two millennia of clinical use.

What that tells me about evidence: **funding and crisis create evidence density, not antiquity**. A 4-year-old formula can outpace a 2000-year-old one on PubMed shelves. This isn't a bug; it's how the system works.

**3. The interaction literature is shockingly thin.**

For every Grade A trial on a TCM intervention, there's roughly 1 paper on its interactions with concomitant Western drugs. The ratio should be inverted in a population where polypharmacy is the rule, not the exception.

This is the actual gap. Not "does this herb work" — that's getting better. But "does this herb work WHILE this patient is on amlodipine, metformin, and a statin" — that's where we're flying blind.

**4. The herbs with the worst safety reputation often have the cleanest pharmacovigilance data.**

附子 (Aconite), 麻黃 (Ephedra), 細辛 (Asarum). The horror cases are real but they're case reports, not RCT signals. The actual issue is **dose, processing method, and prescriber competence**, not the herb itself. The literature here is precise; the public narrative is sloppy.

**5. The single most useful read of the 707 was a Frontiers in Pharmacology paper on Buyang Huanwu Tang for post-stroke recovery.**

Not because of the result (it works). Because of the methodology section. It's the cleanest example I've seen of a TCM trial designed with Western methodological rigour while preserving TCM dosing flexibility (allowing per-patient modifications within a defined herb set). That's the template more TCM trials should follow.

(PMID 35168040 — worth saving even if you never prescribe the formula.)

**6. The biggest evidence gap, by far, is pediatrics.**

Out of 707 entries, fewer than 30 involve patients under 12. The 王琦 9-constitution framework, the formula databases, the safety thresholds — almost all are calibrated to adults. Pediatric TCM is a different discipline operationally, and the evidence base is decades behind adult medicine.

This is where I'd build next, if I were starting from scratch.

→ I trust case-series less than I used to.
→ I assume polypharmacy and ask explicitly, every time.
